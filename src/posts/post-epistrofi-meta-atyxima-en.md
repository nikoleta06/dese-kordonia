<div class="quote">
  <blockquote>"People are disturbed not by things, but by the views they take of them."</blockquote>
  <cite><strong>Epictetus, Enchiridion</strong></cite>
</div>

Almost two thousand years later, this line feels truer to me than ever. Epictetus wasn't talking about running. He was talking about life, about the things that happen to us and the way we think about them. A loss, a failure, an obstacle we didn't see coming. But here we talk about running, and out on the road this line fits like few others.

Often what stops us isn't what happened, but the story we tell ourselves about it. "I've lost my fitness." "It will take months." "I'll never be the same again."

This is an article about how a break from running, even a forced one, doesn't erase what you've built. And about how the hardest part of coming back may not be the body, but the mind.

A few months ago, things had finally started to fall into place. I had just started running again, after almost seven months away because of work, other commitments and some health issues that kept setting me back. Around the same time, I had put the site you're reading right now online.

Two weeks after my first run, and just a few days after the site appeared on Google, I had an accident. It could have been much worse. I got away with a broken arm.

### Three and a half months off running

Running was out of the question. The only thing that made me happy during the day was this site. I thought about what new things to add, I made it better, I worked on its backend. It was my way of staying close to running, even when I couldn't run.

### The return

When I recovered, after three and a half months, I went out again for an easy run. I was sure I'd be worse than ever. I had stayed completely away from exercise and believed it would take me a very long time to find my rhythm and my endurance again.

This was my own path, without a plan from a coach or specialist. I went out every afternoon, five times a week, without pushing myself at all. The first week I ran 1 to 1.5 kilometres. The second, 2 to 2.5. The third, 3 to 3.5.

My approach was simple. I didn't push to run fast. I kept a steady pace and kept checking my watch, so my heart rate stayed under 150. For me that means close to my [aerobic zone](post-zones-palmon-en.html), only barely touching the orange zone on my watch, that is 140 to 150.

### The kilometre I didn't expect

One day that third week, I ran my first kilometre in **4:48**. I didn't push. It just happened, the way it used to. I hadn't run a kilometre at that pace in two years, and my new Garmin beeped for a personal record, because it had never recorded it before.

After the run I realised I hadn't lost as much as I had feared. The fear was bigger than the loss, and the comeback I had imagined so far away had already begun. Epictetus said it long before me.

### You don't start from zero

I'm writing this because I know how easily we believe the opposite. An injury, a hard period, months away from the sport we love, and it feels as if everything has gone wrong. As if we've lost everything we had built.

When I thought about it, [Faith Kipyegon](post-faith-kipyegon-en.html) came to mind. In 2018 she missed a whole season of racing when her daughter was born. She simply needed a little time to find her rhythm again. In the years that followed she won two more Olympic golds and broke world records.

We're not all Kipyegon. But the logic is the same. A break doesn't erase what you've built, and the body remembers more than we think.

If you're in a break like that right now, start small. Don't compare yourself with who you were before. And give your body the chance to surprise you. It surprised me.

{% note %}If you're coming back from an injury, get the OK from your doctor or physiotherapist first.{% endnote %}
