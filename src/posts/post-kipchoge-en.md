**Eliud Kipchoge** is considered by many the greatest marathon runner of all time, with two Olympic marathon golds (Rio 2016, Tokyo 2021). He has said plenty of things that stuck. I picked two of them, plus a few of his habits that work for those of us who run a lot slower.

### "No human is limited"

In 2017, in Monza, Italy, he tried to run a marathon in under two hours and finished in 2:00:25. He was 25 seconds short. Two years later, in Vienna, he tried again and stopped the clock at **1:59:40**. It was a special event, with teams of pacers rotating in and out, so the time doesn't count as an official record. But it showed the two-hour barrier could be broken.

For us, the phrase doesn't have to mean a two-hour marathon. It can mean your first 5K without stopping, or a race you thought you'd never finish. A lot of limits are set by the mind first and the body second.

### "Only the disciplined ones in life are free"

Kipchoge spends his weekdays at the training camp in Kaptagat, Kenya, away from his family and from any comforts. He shares the chores with the other athletes, even the cleaning. For years he has also kept a handwritten log of every training session.

The idea behind the phrase is simple. Once good habits become routine, you don't have to fight yourself every morning to get out the door. You just go.

### The smile in the hard kilometres

Anyone who has watched Kipchoge race has noticed him smiling in the toughest parts. He has explained that he does it to relax and forget the pain. A 2018 study found something similar: runners who smiled ran slightly more economically than those who frowned.

### Always part of the group

Even though he's the biggest name at the camp, he always trains with the group, alongside runners much slower than him, and stays down to earth despite his success. They run together and push each other. If you run with a club or with friends, you know how much easier a hard interval feels when you're not alone.

If you keep one thing from this article, make it the log. A notebook or a note on your phone, with three lines after every run: how far you went, how you felt, what you'd change. In six months you'll have your own story written down.
